# Getting Started

These five steps cover planning and writing a thesis in all four programs (BAIS, BAKS, MAAS, MAIR). Work through them in order when you start, and return to a step when your project changes. Click a step to expand it. Each one ends with an exercise that gives you something concrete to bring to your supervisor, and [Templates & Checklists]({{ '/templates/' | relative_url }}) has working documents to go with them.

---

## The Five Steps {#the-five-steps}

<details id="step-1" markdown="0">
<summary>Step 1: Developing Your Research Question</summary>
<div class="details-content">

<p class="step-intro">Your research question decides which sources you read and how you analyze them.</p>

<div class="step-objectives">
<p>Objectives</p>
<ul>
<li>Know what makes a good research question and how to identify a research gap</li>
<li>Draft a working question for your first supervision meeting</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h4>What makes a strong research question?</h4>

<ul>
<li><strong>Clear</strong>: a reader understands it without further explanation</li>
<li><strong>Focused</strong>: it is narrow enough to answer within your thesis</li>
<li><strong>Relevant</strong>: it connects to an existing scholarly debate</li>
<li><strong>Researchable</strong>: it can be answered with data and methods you can access</li>
</ul>

<h4>Research problem and research gap</h4>

<p>The <strong>research problem</strong> is the broader issue behind your question and the reason it matters, such as an unresolved debate or an accepted view that deserves a fresh look. The <strong>research gap</strong> is the specific hole your thesis fills, such as an understudied case, an untested theory, a missing comparison, or new data.</p>

<div class="tip-box">
<p><strong>Your question will change.</strong> It almost always does as you read more. Start with a working question that gives you direction and expect to revise it.</p>
</div>

<div class="reflection-box">
<p>Ask yourself</p>
<ul>
<li>Which topic interests you most?</li>
<li>What do scholars already know about it, and what don't they know?</li>
<li>Can you answer the question with the data and methods available to you?</li>
<li>Is the scope realistic for your program's word count?</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="exercise-box">
<p>Exercise</p>
<p>Draft a working research question (1–2 sentences). Then write 150–250 words that explain the following.</p>
<ol>
<li>The research problem and the gap your question addresses</li>
<li>How you might begin to answer it</li>
</ol>
<p>Bring this to your first supervision meeting.</p>
</div>

</div>
</details>

<details id="step-2" markdown="0">
<summary>Step 2: Data and Sources</summary>
<div class="details-content">

<p class="step-intro">This step covers where to look for evidence and how to manage what you find.</p>

<div class="step-objectives">
<p>Objectives</p>
<ul>
<li>Distinguish primary from secondary sources and apply the FAIR principles</li>
<li>Draft a preliminary data collection plan</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h4>Primary vs. secondary sources</h4>

<p><strong>Primary sources</strong> are the original materials you analyze, such as policy documents, news articles, speeches, literary works, statistical datasets, survey results, interview transcripts, archival records, and social media content. <strong>Secondary sources</strong> are scholarly works that interpret such material, including journal articles, monographs, book chapters, and review essays. Most theses use both, and the balance depends on your question and method.</p>

<h4>The FAIR Principles</h4>

<table>
<thead><tr><th>Principle</th><th>In practice</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>Findable</strong></td><td>Organize and label your sources with reference management software (Zotero, Mendeley)</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Accessible</strong></td><td>Make sure you can legally and practically access the sources you need</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Interoperable</strong></td><td>Use standard formats and consistent naming conventions</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Reusable</strong></td><td>Document your sources, methods, and any transformations so others can use them</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<div class="reflection-box">
<p>Ask yourself</p>
<ul>
<li>What data do you need to answer your research question?</li>
<li>Is it available and accessible, and does it require language skills?</li>
<li>Does the project raise ethical issues, such as human participants or sensitive data? See the <a href="{{ '/ethics/' | relative_url }}">Ethics &amp; AI</a> page.</li>
<li>How will you collect the data and keep it organized throughout the project?</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="exercise-box">
<p>Exercise</p>
<p>Write a data collection plan (300–500 words) that covers these points.</p>
<ol>
<li>The primary and secondary sources you will use</li>
<li>How you will find and access them</li>
<li>How the plan follows the FAIR principles</li>
<li>Likely problems or limits in data access</li>
</ol>
</div>

</div>
</details>

<details id="step-3" markdown="0">
<summary>Step 3: Conducting a Literature Review</summary>
<div class="details-content">

<p class="step-intro">A literature review maps what scholars have established on your topic and shows what they have left open.</p>

<div class="step-objectives">
<p>Objectives</p>
<ul>
<li>Connect the literature to your research question and gap</li>
<li>Start an annotated bibliography</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h4>What a literature review does</h4>

<p>A literature review summarizes existing work on your topic and identifies what remains overlooked or contested. It then places your thesis within that scholarship and justifies your question and approach.</p>

<div class="tip-box">
<p><strong>Stay focused.</strong> Engage closely with the few debates most relevant to your question instead of cataloging everything written on the topic.</p>
</div>

<h4>How to organize your review</h4>
<p>You can organize a review by theme (the most common choice), by chronology, by method, or around competing theoretical perspectives. Many reviews combine more than one of these.</p>

<h4>Building your review step by step</h4>
<ol>
<li>Choose search terms for your topic, question, and field</li>
<li>Search widely in Google Scholar, JSTOR, Web of Science, and the specialist databases for your field</li>
<li>Read abstracts and introductions first to judge relevance</li>
<li>For each source, note the main argument, methods, findings, and relation to your research</li>
<li>Group sources into themes or competing positions</li>
<li>State what the existing literature does not address</li>
</ol>

<div class="reflection-box">
<p>Ask yourself</p>
<ul>
<li>What are the main debates, and where do scholars agree or disagree?</li>
<li>Which methods have been used, and which are missing?</li>
<li>Are there geographic or temporal blind spots in the scholarship?</li>
<li>What does your question address that the literature has not?</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="exercise-box">
<p>Exercise</p>
<p><strong>Part A:</strong> Create an annotated bibliography of 8–12 key sources. For each, write 3–4 sentences on its main argument or finding and its methods, and on how it relates to your research question.</p>
<p><strong>Part B:</strong> Outline your literature review, organized by whichever principle best fits your sources.</p>
</div>

</div>
</details>

<details id="step-4" markdown="0">
<summary>Step 4: Building Your Analytical Framework</summary>
<div class="details-content">

<p class="step-intro">Your analytical framework links your research question to your findings.</p>

<div class="step-objectives">
<p>Objectives</p>
<ul>
<li>Select and justify methods that fit your question and data</li>
<li>Turn that justification into a methods outline</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h4>How it fits together</h4>

<p>The framework should follow from your literature review. The gap you identified motivates your research design, and your methods should suit the type of data you are working with. The framework states <strong>what</strong> you are analyzing (your data or cases), <strong>how</strong> you will analyze it, and <strong>why</strong> that approach suits your question.</p>

<h4>Common approaches</h4>

<p><strong>Qualitative methods</strong></p>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Method</th><th>When to use it</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Thematic analysis</td><td>Identifying patterns and themes in textual data</td></tr>
<tr><td>Content analysis</td><td>Consistent coding and categorization of textual material</td></tr>
<tr><td>Discourse analysis</td><td>Examining how language constructs meaning</td></tr>
<tr><td>Case study analysis</td><td>In-depth examination of one or more cases</td></tr>
<tr><td>Process tracing</td><td>Tracking causal mechanisms through detailed evidence</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p><strong>Quantitative methods</strong></p>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Method</th><th>When to use it</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Descriptive statistics</td><td>Summarizing patterns in numerical data</td></tr>
<tr><td>Inferential statistics</td><td>Testing hypotheses about relationships between variables</td></tr>
<tr><td>Comparative analysis</td><td>Comparison across cases or datasets</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<div class="tip-box">
<p><strong>Mixed methods</strong> combine qualitative and quantitative approaches. They can strengthen an analysis but add complexity, so discuss with your supervisor whether they suit your project.</p>
</div>

<div class="info-box">
<p>The <a href="{{ '/methods/' | relative_url }}">Methods Guide</a> covers comparative case studies, process tracing, framing analysis, discourse analysis, and corpus building in more depth.</p>
</div>

<h4>Justifying your choices</h4>
<p>Your thesis should explain why you chose your method, how it suits your question and data, what its limitations are and how you account for them, and how the analysis is structured. The exercise below is a first draft of that explanation.</p>

<div class="reflection-box">
<p>Ask yourself</p>
<ul>
<li>What type of analysis does your research question require?</li>
<li>Which methods does your field usually use for this type of question?</li>
<li>Do you have the skills and resources to apply your chosen method?</li>
<li>How will you structure your findings chapter?</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="exercise-box">
<p>Exercise</p>
<p>Write a methods outline (300–500 words) that covers these points.</p>
<ol>
<li>Your research design (qualitative, quantitative, or mixed)</li>
<li>Your specific method(s) and why they are appropriate</li>
<li>How you will structure the analysis</li>
<li>Key limitations and how you plan to address them</li>
</ol>
</div>

</div>
</details>

<details id="step-5" markdown="0">
<summary>Step 5: Writing and Reporting Findings</summary>
<div class="details-content">

<p class="step-intro">The findings chapter presents what your analysis shows. This step covers how to structure it and how to plan the writing of the whole thesis.</p>

<div class="step-objectives">
<p>Objectives</p>
<ul>
<li>Structure your findings and connect them to your research question and the literature</li>
<li>Develop a practical writing strategy</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h4>Structuring your findings</h4>

<table>
<thead><tr><th>Structure</th><th>How it works</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>By theme</strong></td><td>Group results around the key themes from your analysis</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>By case</strong></td><td>Present findings for each case separately, then compare</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>By sub-question</strong></td><td>Address each research sub-question in turn</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Chronologically</strong></td><td>Trace developments over time</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Whatever structure you choose, each section should do the following.</p>
<ol>
<li>State what you found</li>
<li>Present the evidence (quotes, data, examples)</li>
<li>Analyze what the evidence means</li>
<li>Connect it to your research question and existing literature</li>
</ol>

<h4>From findings to conclusions</h4>

<p>Your conclusion summarizes the main findings in relation to the research question and states what your research adds to the field. It also acknowledges what you could not do and why, and names the questions that remain open.</p>

<div class="tip-box">
<p><strong>Do not introduce new evidence or arguments in the conclusion.</strong> It should synthesize what you have already presented.</p>
</div>

<h4>Practical writing strategies</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Start with what you know best</strong>: you do not have to write chapters in order, and many students begin with the literature review or methods.</li>
<li><strong>Write regularly</strong>: steady daily or weekly sessions work better than marathon sessions before a deadline.</li>
<li><strong>Revise iteratively</strong>: write a rough first draft, then improve it.</li>
<li><strong>Get feedback early</strong>: bring drafts to supervision meetings, where early feedback is most useful.</li>
<li><strong>Cite as you write</strong>: use reference management software (Zotero, Mendeley) from the start.</li>
</ul>

<h4>Formatting</h4>
<p>Citation styles vary by program, so check your <a href="{{ '/#find-your-program' | relative_url }}">program overview</a> and apply your style <strong>consistently</strong> throughout the thesis. The <a href="https://www.library.universiteitleiden.nl/students/citing">Leiden University Library citing page</a> has citation guides.</p>

<div class="reflection-box">
<p>Ask yourself</p>
<ul>
<li>How will you organize your findings?</li>
<li>What is the key evidence for each of your main points?</li>
<li>How do your findings relate to the literature you reviewed?</li>
<li>What does your research contribute, and what limitations should you acknowledge?</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="exercise-box">
<p>Exercise</p>
<p><strong>Part A:</strong> Outline your findings chapter, with section headings and brief notes on the evidence for each section.</p>
<p><strong>Part B:</strong> Write a rough draft of your conclusion (500–750 words) that covers your findings, contributions, limitations, and future research.</p>
</div>

</div>
</details>

## Next Steps

When you have finished these five steps you will have a refined research question, a plan for collecting data, an annotated bibliography and an outline of your literature review, a methods outline, and a plan for your findings and conclusion. Bring these to your supervision meetings so that your supervisor can help you refine them and meet your deadline.

Your program page ([BAIS]({{ '/bais/' | relative_url }}), [BAKS]({{ '/baks/' | relative_url }}), [MAAS]({{ '/maas/' | relative_url }}), [MAIR]({{ '/mair/' | relative_url }})) has the requirements and deadlines for your thesis. The BAKS page also links to the Thesis Seminar site.
