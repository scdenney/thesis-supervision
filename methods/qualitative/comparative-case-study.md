# Comparative Case Study Design

Comparative case study design is for questions that need more than one case but still require knowledge of each case. The comparison works only when you choose the cases for reasons you can defend. The method is common in political science, international relations, and area studies.

---

## What Is Comparative Case Study Design?

You examine two or more cases in a structured comparison, holding some factors constant while others vary. That controlled variation gives the comparison its analytical, and usually causal, leverage.

The foundations go back to John Stuart Mill's ([1843](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_System_of_Logic)) *methods of agreement and difference*. [Lijphart (1971)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1955513) treated comparison as a distinct research strategy in political science, and [Przeworski and Teune (1970)](https://archive.org/details/logicofcomparati0000prze) developed the most similar and most different systems designs that still shape theses and published research.

<div class="info-box" markdown="1">

**Comparison needs a stated rationale.** Two separate case chapters do not make a comparative thesis. The analytical framework has to say why these cases belong together and how comparing them answers the research question.

</div>

---

## Key Concepts

### Most Similar Systems Design (MSSD)

MSSD selects cases that are similar in background conditions but differ on the outcome. If the cases share most features and still diverge, the features they do not share become the leading candidates for explanation and, where conditions permit, a causal claim.

A comparison of democratic backsliding in Hungary and Poland follows this logic. Both are post-communist EU member states with similar institutional starting points, yet their trajectories after 2015 diverged. MSSD corresponds to Mill's *method of difference*. It is the more common design in comparative theses because it controls for context without statistical methods, although quantitative studies often use an MSSD design too (see the examples below).

### Most Different Systems Design (MDSD)

MDSD does the opposite. It selects cases that differ on most background conditions but share the outcome, so the factors they have in common deserve the attention. A study of why protest movements in Chile, Hong Kong, and Sudan peaked in 2019 would follow this logic, looking for common conditions across very different cases. MDSD corresponds to Mill's *method of agreement* and is most useful for identifying factors that operate similarly across diverse contexts.

### Case Selection

Case selection is the most consequential methodological decision in a comparative thesis. [Seawright and Gerring (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1065912907313077) set out several principled selection techniques, each serving a different purpose.

| Selection strategy | Purpose | Good fit |
|--------------------|---------|-------------|
| **Most similar** | Isolate what explains different outcomes | You have cases with shared context but divergent results |
| **Most different** | Identify common causes across contexts | You have cases with different contexts but the same outcome |
| **Typical** | Represent a broader population | You want findings that generalize |
| **Deviant** | Challenge or refine existing theory | A case contradicts what theory predicts |
| **Extreme** | Examine an unusual value on a key variable | A case is an outlier worth understanding |

<div class="tip-box" markdown="1">

**For BA students**, a two-case MSSD design is _usually_ the safest choice if you want a truly comparative design. Select cases with a clear shared context and a meaningful difference in outcome. **For MA students**, a third case can work if the word limit leaves room for the comparison.

</div>

## When to Use It

Use a comparative case study design if your research question asks **why** outcomes vary across cases or what explains a shared outcome in different contexts. You also need a set of cases that allows inference from their similarity or difference, and enough qualitative evidence for a within-case analysis. The aim is a **causal or explanatory claim**, even a tentative one.

It is a poor fit for a mainly descriptive project or for cases with no plausible basis for structured comparison. If the question is really about the internal mechanics of one case, consider [process tracing]({{ '/methods/qualitative/process-tracing' | relative_url }}), which also pairs well with comparison.

## How to Apply It

### Step 1: Formulate a comparative research question

Your question should invite comparison by asking why an outcome varies across cases or what explains a shared outcome in different contexts.

**Weak.** "What is China's Belt and Road Initiative?"

**Stronger.** "Why have some Southeast Asian countries embraced China's Belt and Road Initiative while others have resisted it?"

The stronger version names variation in an outcome (embrace vs. resistance) across a defined set of cases (Southeast Asian countries). It also invites causal explanation, which is risky for a student thesis.

### Step 2: Select your cases with an explicit rationale

State whether you are using MSSD, MDSD, or another selection logic. Then explain *why* your cases fit it by naming the background conditions they share or differ on and the outcome you study. Students from area studies are usually well placed to know the contextual background an MSSD design depends on.

Build a case selection table early. For an MSSD design, it might look like this.

| Factor | Case A | Case B |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Region | East Asia | East Asia |
| Regime type | Democracy | Democracy |
| Economic level | High income | High income |
| Colonial history | Japanese colonialism | Japanese colonialism |
| **Outcome (DV)** | **Persistent ethnic nationalism** | **Weakened ethnic nationalism** |

A table this simple cannot justify a design on its own, but it makes the logic visible. The shared conditions are what you control for, and the divergent outcome is what you want to explain. You are looking for a condition that _varies_ between the two cases and may explain the divergent outcome. The [Key Readings](#key-readings) cover this in more detail.

### Step 3: Build your analytical framework

Your analytical framework connects the research question to your case selection and theoretical expectations. It should specify two things.

- The **dependent variable**, meaning the _outcome_ you are explaining and how you operationalize it
- The **independent variables or explanatory factors** drawn from the literature review, with their **observable implications** (what you would expect to see in each case if a given explanation holds)

At the BA level, a clear statement of these elements is sufficient. At the MA level, also discuss why alternative explanations are less persuasive and how your framework builds on or departs from existing theory. For general guidance, see [Getting Started, Step 4: Building Your Analytical Framework]({{ '/getting-started/#step-4' | relative_url }}).

### Step 4: Conduct within-case analysis

Before you compare, you need to understand each case on its own terms. That means careful, in-depth collection and analysis of evidence on the variables in your analytical framework. Whether that evidence comes from primary documents, interviews, secondary scholarship, data, or another source depends on the case. Write up each case in parallel, asking the same questions about the same variables in the same order, or the cross-case element is lost.

### Step 5: Conduct cross-case comparison

Compare the cases along each dimension of the framework, in the same order for every case. Ask where they align and diverge, what accounts for the divergence, and whether the patterns support or challenge your expectations. A comparison matrix helps, summarizing for each explanatory factor the evidence from each case and whether it supports the expected relationship.

### Step 6: Draw conclusions with appropriate scope

State what your comparison shows and how far the claim extends. A two-case comparison supports conditional generalizations, such as "under these conditions, this factor appears to matter," so take particular care with a causal (*why*) claim (see [pitfall 6](#common-pitfalls)). Say what your findings contribute to the debate from your literature review and note the limitations openly.

<div class="exercise-box" markdown="1">

**Exercise. Case selection justification**

If you are considering a comparative design, prepare a one-page case selection memo before a supervision meeting, ideally the first. Include (1) your research question, (2) the design logic (MSSD, MDSD, or other), (3) a case selection table listing the key background conditions and outcome for each case, and (4) a short explanation of why these cases give explanatory traction on your question.

</div>

## Structuring Your Thesis

Chapter titles vary, but a comparative case study thesis should map to this logic.

| Chapter | Content | What it does |
|---------|---------|--------------|
| **1. Introduction** | Research question, puzzle, contribution, roadmap | Sets up the comparison and why it matters |
| **2. Literature review** | Existing scholarship and the gap | Shows where your thesis intervenes |
| **3. Analytical framework** | Theory, variables, case selection rationale, method | Explains *how* you will answer the question and *why* these cases |
| **4. Case 1 findings** | Structured analysis of the first case | Applies the framework, organized by your variables |
| **5. Case 2 findings** | Structured analysis of the second case | Applies the framework in parallel structure |
| **6. Cross-case comparison** | Structured comparison across cases | Identifies patterns and tests expectations |
| **7. Conclusion** | Answer, contributions, limitations | States what you found and what it means for the debate |

- **The analytical framework chapter must justify your case selection.** Explain which design logic you use and what the comparison is designed to show. Examiners look for this.
- **With three cases**, give each its own chapter or group two similar cases together and give the third its own. Discuss the structure with your supervisor.

<div class="tip-box" markdown="1">

**BA students** typically write two case chapters plus a cross-case comparison within a 10,000-word thesis. **MA students** have room for a third case or deeper within-case analysis. Either way, protect space for the cross-case comparison, where the design is judged and the highest marks are earned. For what examiners look for, see [Assessment Standards, Reaching Conclusions]({{ '/assessment-standards/#reaching-conclusions' | relative_url }}).

</div>

## Example from the Literature

The three studies below all use the South Korea–Taiwan MSSD. Read them for how they handle case selection, parallel structure, and cross-case analysis.

[Denney, Steinhardt, and Bhowmick (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1080/13537113.2026.2616954) ask why citizens in these two divided societies overstate national pride in surveys, and how conformity pressure differs across the cases. Both are East Asian democracies with advanced economies, legacies of Japanese colonial rule, and American-aligned Cold War development. Both democratized in the late twentieth century, and in both an unresolved rival state (the PRC and North Korea) shapes domestic politics and identity. The authors name these shared conditions, explain why each works as a control, and identify the difference the comparison isolates. Drawing on experimental survey findings, they argue that the difference is how national identity norms diverged during democratization itself. Both cases are analyzed with the same framework, so the study can identify a shared phenomenon (identity conformity) while explaining variation in how it operates. Your analytical framework chapter needs the same kind of justification.

Hur ([2022](https://www.cornellpress.cornell.edu/book/9781501763649/narratives-of-civic-duty/)), *Narratives of Civic Duty: How National Stories Shape Democracy in Asia* (Cornell UP), builds the same comparison around the **state-citizen linkage** (the historicized bond between a national people and their state). Citizens are likely to do their civic duty (military service, voting, paying taxes) when they consider the state a representation of their nation, and national narratives are important in creating this linkage. In South Korea, the experiences of Japanese colonialism, the Korean War, and military dictatorship created a narrative of Koreans as a distinct people who fought hard to create their own state, which they feel obliged to sustain because it is *theirs*. In Taiwan, however, the majority Taiwanese-origin population long associated the state with KMT mainlander rule, an outside imposition. Even after democratization, national attachment there does not translate into civic duty with the same force.

[Jo (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1177/00104140251369326) combines comparative design with [process tracing]({{ '/methods/qualitative/process-tracing' | relative_url }}). Democratization, Jo argues, empowers new **storytelling elites** (rival politicians, dissident intellectuals, and civil society leaders) to challenge the state's national narrative. They can contest its **bottom-line premise**, the foundational claim about who the national people are, or only its **sideline elements**. Both cases inherited state-imposed narratives of national oneness. South Korean elites contested sideline elements, so democracy *entrenched* the "One Korea" narrative. Taiwanese elites challenged the premise itself, and democracy *displaced* the "One China" narrative. The study pairs a structured comparison of what differs between the cases with interpretive process tracing of *how* the divergence unfolded in each, the kind of interpretive care common in area studies and the humanities.

<div class="reflection-box" markdown="1">

**Reflection. Learning from published work**

Find one published comparative study in your topic area (your supervisor can suggest one) and note (1) its case selection logic, (2) how it structures the within-case and cross-case analysis, and (3) the scope conditions it places on the findings. Bring your notes to supervision.

</div>

## Common Pitfalls

**1. Selecting cases on the dependent variable without variation.**
If both cases have the same outcome in a design meant to explain variation, you can only describe commonalities. Make sure your design has inferential power. That usually means variation on the outcome (MSSD) or in the background conditions (MDSD), where the target is the shared cause.

**2. Writing two separate case studies instead of a comparison.**
Without parallel case chapters and a dedicated cross-case chapter, the thesis is two case studies placed side by side.

**3. Choosing cases for convenience.**
"I speak Korean and Japanese" makes a comparison *feasible*, but it is a feasibility statement. The justification must also say why comparing these cases helps answer your research question.

**4. Failing to specify the analytical framework before analyzing the cases.**
Define your variables and expectations in their own chapter, before the case chapters. Otherwise you risk cherry-picking evidence, and the reader cannot tell whether you are testing your expectations or confirming them.

**5. Weak cross-case comparison.**
A cross-case chapter that restates the case chapters adds nothing. It should identify patterns and weigh competing explanations. Examiners often form their strongest impression of your analytical ability here.

**6. Overclaiming from a small number of cases.**
Two or three cases cannot prove a general theory and may not establish a causal connection even within the cases studied. Write "these findings suggest that, under conditions of X, factor Y may contribute to outcome Z," and avoid language that claims proof.

**7. Ignoring alternative explanations.**
Weigh your preferred explanation against plausible alternatives and say why it is more persuasive. This matters most at the MA level.

## Key Readings

**Foundational works:**

- Mill, J. S. (1843). *A System of Logic*. John W. Parker. The original formulation of the methods of agreement and difference.
- [Lijphart, A. (1971)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1955513). "Comparative politics and the comparative method." *American Political Science Review*, 65(3), 682–693. The classic statement of comparison as a distinct research strategy.
- Przeworski, A., & Teune, H. (1970). *The Logic of Comparative Social Inquiry*. Wiley-Interscience. Develops the most similar and most different systems designs.

**On case selection and design:**

- [Seawright, J., & Gerring, J. (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1065912907313077). "Case selection techniques in case study research: A menu of qualitative and quantitative options." *Political Research Quarterly*, 61(2), 294–308. Essential reading on case selection.
- George, A. L., & Bennett, A. (2005). *Case Studies and Theory Development in the Social Sciences*. MIT Press. A guide to structured, focused comparison and theory-building from cases.
- [Seawright, J. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781316160831). *Multi-Method Social Science: Combining Qualitative and Quantitative Tools*. Cambridge UP. Useful for MA students combining comparative case design with other methods.

**Applied examples:**

- [Denney, S., Steinhardt, H. C., & Bhowmick, L. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1080/13537113.2026.2616954). "Identity conformity in Taiwan and South Korea: Why citizens in divided societies are pressured to overstate national pride." *Nationalism and Ethnic Politics*. MSSD comparison of identity conformity in two divided-nation democracies.
- Hur, A. (2022). *Narratives of Civic Duty: How National Stories Shape Democracy in Asia*. Cornell UP. National narratives and civic behavior in Asian democracies.
- [Jo, E. A. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1177/00104140251369326). "Storytelling Elites and the Remaking of Nationhood in Democratic South Korea and Taiwan." *Comparative Political Studies*. MSSD comparison combined with interpretive process tracing.

<div class="info-box" markdown="1">

Start with [Seawright and Gerring (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1065912907313077) for case selection and George and Bennett (2005) for structured comparison.

</div>

## Related Methods

- **[Process Tracing]({{ '/methods/qualitative/process-tracing' | relative_url }}).** Use it when the comparison shows which factor matters but the thesis still has to show the path from cause to outcome inside each case. [Seawright (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781316160831) discusses how the two combine.

- **[Building a Corpus]({{ '/methods/building-a-corpus' | relative_url }}).** Use it when each case rests on documents, speeches, policy records, or media coverage. Define the source base for each case the same way, or uneven evidence will drive the comparison.

Assessment criteria for method choice are listed under [Assessment Standards, Application of Knowledge]({{ '/assessment-standards/#application-of-knowledge' | relative_url }}). If the comparison involves interviews, vulnerable groups, or sensitive national contexts, check [Ethics]({{ '/ethics/' | relative_url }}) before collecting material.
